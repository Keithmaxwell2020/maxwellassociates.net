The King Maker Rule: How Washington Became the Gatekeeper of the World's AI Computing

A rule released Monday makes U.S. permission the price of serving Chinese AI customers from data centers anywhere in the world. Behind the Nvidia drama, this is the real fight, and most people missed it.

Tuesday, November 17, 2026 · Keith J. Maxwell

While headlines followed Jensen Huang's resignation and a Cabinet feud, something more lasting happened in a regulatory notice. On Monday, Nov. 16, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) released an interim final rule titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors." It is scheduled to be published in the Federal Register and take effect on Nov. 19.

In plain terms, U.S. control over an advanced AI chip no longer ends at the factory door or the border. It follows the chip to whoever uses its computing power. In my view, that is the historic part.

What the rule actually does

According to the law firm Nixon Peabody and CNBC:

  • Data centers anywhere. A license is required to ship advanced chips or servers to a data center anywhere in the world if the shipper knows it will provide more than 12 million "aggregate TPP" of computing to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other restricted countries, including through linked facilities or a middleman. Existing arrangements get 90 days to get a license.
  • Know your customer. Exporters are expected to get information on the foreign data center's own customer checks, down to indirect ownership and ultimate parent companies. A data center that refuses or can't answer is treated as a red flag.
  • Five named countries. Advanced chips now need a license to go to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil. U.S.-headquartered buyers are exempt if the exporter files monthly reports, and the Commerce secretary can add countries.
  • Old rules cleaned up. The rule formally rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule while keeping the worldwide reach of the advanced-computing "foreign direct product" rule.

BIS said the rule was "written by and at the direction of" the Treasury Department and the National Security Council. Politico reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed it through over Commerce's objections.

A supply chain that runs on U.S. permission

A top Nvidia chip is designed in the United States and made mostly by TSMC in Taiwan, with Blackwell production also under way at TSMC's plant in Arizona. Contract manufacturers, led by Taiwanese firms such as Foxconn and Wistron, then build the chips into servers and racks in Taiwan, Mexico and, increasingly, Texas (Nvidia; CNA).

Indonesia's role is as a destination, not an assembly hub. Firmus and DayOne are building a 360-megawatt Nvidia "AI factory" on Batam for up to 170,000 Nvidia chips (Jakarta Post). Indonesia is now on the license list, and its police chief confirmed Tuesday that the FBI asked for help investigating Megaspeed, the Singapore firm at the center of the Huang scandal (Indonesia Business Post).

What ties the whole chain together is the foreign direct product rule. It extends U.S. export rules to foreign-made items produced with U.S. technology, which is why TSMC follows Washington's chip rules. The new rule keeps that reach, including over a controlled chip built into a foreign-made server, with no exemption for small U.S. content. A chip made in Taiwan, racked in Mexico and switched on in Batam still answers to Washington's question: who is the customer?

Gatekeeper, not yet global licensor

To be fair, the rule does not make everyone in the world get a U.S. license to use AI. Most buyers in Europe, Japan, Taiwan and the U.S. need none unless they serve China-headquartered companies at scale. Taiwan and Mexico, where much of the hardware is made, are not on the list.

The irony: Biden's AI Diffusion Rule, which would have sorted countries into tiers, was closer to a true worldwide licensing system. Trump's Commerce Department scrapped it in May 2025, saying it would have "undermined U.S. diplomatic relations with dozens of countries." Monday's rule is narrower, aimed at China and the routes to it. But the machinery is new: ownership checks that run to the ultimate parent company, a country list the secretary can expand, and a warning of "consequences" for countries that "tolerate evasion." Limited for now, but expandable.

The real fight: open access vs. controlled access

Huang argued the other way for years. In May 2025 he called U.S. export controls "a failure" that cut Nvidia's China share from 95% to 50% and pushed Beijing to build its own chips (CNBC). "The American tech stack should be the world standard," he told Yahoo Finance that August, "and that includes China."

The hawks' case is that controls only work if they follow the computing, not just the boxes. Monday's rule is a clear win for that camp, and it came days after Huang left Nvidia. My view is that this, more than the affair, is the real fight between Huang and the administration.

It isn't settled. Trump said he understood "both sides" and added, "I'll make a decision" (Washington Post). China called the rule "typical economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction" and vowed countermeasures (Xinhua). Trump is at the ASEAN summit in Manila, set to sign a trade deal with Thailand, one of the five listed countries, before the APEC summit in Shenzhen. Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick are both with him (CBS News).

Commentary: King Trump, King Maker

What follows is my opinion. Put the pieces together. The world's most valuable computing runs on chips that Washington controls from design to data center, and access now depends on customer checks that Washington defines and countries that Washington can add to a list. King Trump now becomes a global King Maker for high technology. Anyone who wants to build AI at scale will need to stay in Washington's good graces.

A king maker can also overreach. Push too hard and customers look for alternatives, which was exactly Huang's warning. Whether this power keeps allies close or drives them to other suppliers may be the defining technology question of the decade.

What to watch

  • Federal Register publication on Nov. 19, which starts the 90-day license clock.
  • Whether Commerce adds countries or Trump softens the rule in China.
  • Nvidia's earnings on Nov. 17, and what interim CEO Colette Kress says about licenses.
  • Congress: the Remote Access Security Act (H.R. 2683) passed the House 369 to 22 on Jan. 12 and sits in Senate Banking (Congress.gov).
