The Loophole Behind Nvidia's Leadership Crisis

Jensen Huang's exit began as a personal scandal. For investors, the bigger story may be what Washington does next about Chinese companies renting AI computing power across borders.

Saturday, November 14, 2026 · Keith J. Maxwell

Most coverage of Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia's chief executive has focused on the personal side: an affair, a suspected spy, and a president who stood by his friend. Investors may want to look past that to a policy question that has been building for more than a year.

U.S. export controls stop China from buying Nvidia's most advanced chips. They do not stop Chinese companies from renting those chips' computing power from data centers in other countries. The Huang saga has given new energy to the people in Washington who want to close that gap, and closing it would change the economics of cloud computing.

That matters for anyone who owns chipmakers, cloud providers, or the new class of U.S. companies that rent out AI capacity, SpaceX among them.

How we got here: a timeline

Below are the main dated reference points. Several key claims, above all that Alice Huang was working for Chinese intelligence, are allegations that remain unproven. China denies them, and the U.S. Justice Department says it has not reached a conclusion.

  • Oct. 9, 2025: The New York Times reports that U.S. officials are investigating whether Singapore-based Megaspeed, an Nvidia partner led by Alice Huang, helped Chinese customers get advanced Nvidia chips. Singapore police also investigate the company, CNBC reported. There has been no public finding that Megaspeed broke the rules. Alice Huang and Jensen Huang are not related.
  • 2025 to February 2026: Singapore investigates Alice Huang over false declarations in server purchases and related offenses, and seizes her passport. She leaves Singapore in February 2026 and is barred from returning. On Nov. 12, Singapore says there was insufficient evidence of any offense, that the probe was not about espionage, and that it got only "limited" help from the U.S. (Reuters via Investing.com; Reuters via Economic Times)
  • Spring 2026: Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversees a classified memo describing intelligence that China's Ministry of State Security had recruited Alice Huang to target Nvidia's CEO, according to the Times. Reuters has not verified this. (Investing.com)
  • May 13, 2026: Short seller Culper Research publishes "NVIDIA: The China Problem." It argues that Nvidia's China business had moved through Southeast Asia rather than disappeared, and it calls the CEO's relationship with Alice Huang "uncomfortably close." (TechCrunch)
  • May 2026: Rubio briefs President Trump about the intelligence aboard Air Force One on the way to Beijing, the Times reported on Nov. 10. Huang, a last-minute addition to the delegation, boarded the plane during a layover in Alaska, CNN reported.
  • Oct. 8, 2026: Trump presents Huang with the National Medal of Science. (Science; Reuters timeline)
  • Oct. 29, 2026: The Times reports that U.S. intelligence officials suspected Alice Huang of acting for Chinese intelligence. On Nov. 6 it reports the memo's assessment that the Ministry of State Security had recruited her. (El País)
  • Nov. 8: Huang acknowledges an extramarital affair without naming the woman. Nvidia's board opens an independent investigation into whether he was the "target of a foreign intelligence effort." (AP; Lianhe Zaobao)
  • Nov. 9: Trump says the FBI is looking into the matter and that he believes Huang's statement that he knew nothing about any spying. China's foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun calls the spy claims "groundless" and "smears." (CNN via WISH-TV; CCTV)
  • Nov. 10: The Information reports that Huang's ties to Alice Huang helped bring about at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip sales to Chinese firms. Reuters has not independently confirmed this. (Reuters timeline)
  • Nov. 12: Nvidia announces that Huang has resigned, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO and Harvey C. Jones becomes the board's first chair. The same day, Attorney General Todd Blanche says the Justice Department has not concluded whether Alice Huang was a spy, and CNN reports disagreement inside the government about how strong the evidence is. Reuters reports that Chinese AI firms are rushing to rent overseas Nvidia capacity. (Economic Times; Lianhe Zaobao)
  • Nov. 12 to 13: Foreign ministry spokeswoman Mao Ning tells Washington to "stop fabricating" spy narratives as a pretext for technology curbs. She also says China opposes the "abuse of export control measures," including proposals to block remote access. (MFA, Nov. 12; MFA, Nov. 13)

The tension: a president's friend and a policy gap

Trump and Huang were close. Trump called him "an amazing man" when he presented the medal and kept relying on his advice on AI, CNN reported. In December 2025 the administration cleared Nvidia's H200 chips for sale to China over objections from hawks in both parties, according to AFP.

On the other side are national-security officials and lawmakers who have long been uneasy about Nvidia's approach. Semafor describes a years-long ideological fight between these hawks and Huang's camp, which argued that spreading U.S. technology worldwide was the better strategy.

The following is analysis, not an established fact. The policy dispute comes down to a gap in the rules. Nvidia can legally sell physical chips to non-Chinese buyers in Southeast Asia. Those buyers can then legally rent computing time to Chinese companies.

"The U.S. export control regime controls physical AI chips. It does not cover remote access to those chips," Cassia King of the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy told CNBC in August. She said a Thai facility reportedly serving Moonshot AI was legal "so long as Moonshot isn't actually buying and owning the physical hardware directly." In July, White House official Michael Kratsios accused Moonshot of using Nvidia GB300 chips through that Thai facility.

CNBC also reported that ByteDance worked with Singapore-based cloud provider Aolani to use computing capacity in Malaysia. Aolani says its customers have no ownership of or physical access to the chips and that it complies with all regulations.

Nothing here shows wrongdoing by Nvidia, and the company has said it has no reason to believe national security was compromised. The critics' argument is different: the rules were written to keep frontier AI training out of China's reach, and renting compute across borders defeats that purpose.

Culper Research went further, alleging financial links between Megaspeed and an Alibaba subsidiary. Culper profits when the stock falls, and its allegations about chip diversion have not been independently verified. Founder Christian Lamarco told MarketWatch he would not say whether the firm is still short.

The regulatory outlook for cloud compute

The main legislative vehicle is the Remote Access Security Act. It would let the Commerce Department regulate remote cloud access to controlled chips. The House passed it 369 to 22 in January. It has not moved in the Senate.

Even if it becomes law, regulators would still have to write a rule. King told CNBC the Bureau of Industry and Security could act in "a matter of days" with White House backing. The hard part, she said, is deciding which compute is covered, who is barred, and how to run "a robust know-your-customer scheme."

Momentum has picked up. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer has pushed several chip bills. House China committee chair John Moolenaar wants export curbs added to the defense bill. Democrats have promised investigations once they take control of Congress in January. Commerce staff have pushed for remote-access curbs, but The Information reported that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick won't act without Trump's go-ahead (AFP via France 24). Trump's veto is still the biggest variable.

Who could win, and who could lose:

  • Southeast Asian "neoclouds" serving Chinese clients face the most direct risk: lost customers, compliance costs, and possible licensing. Reuters' report of Chinese firms rushing to lock in capacity before any rule arrives suggests that the market itself sees a risk.
  • U.S. "clean" providers could gain. One example is SpaceX's AI compute business, which runs the Colossus data centers around Memphis. Its customers on record are Anthropic ($1.25 billion a month) and Google ($920 million a month), plus an unnamed customer at about $1.1 billion a month starting in December. No Chinese customers have been reported. KYC rules would raise costs for everyone, but they would raise them most for providers whose customers are harder to verify.
  • Large U.S. hyperscalers would carry compliance work. They are also the companies best equipped to absorb it.

Why Nvidia's numbers may hold up

None of this means Nvidia's near-term results are in trouble. Since the Culper report the stock is down more than 20%, according to MarketWatch, but the fundamentals tell a different story.

In August, Kress told analysts there was "no China data center compute revenue in our forward outlook." Nvidia guided to about $108 billion in third-quarter revenue, plus or minus 2%. It also forecast roughly 70% revenue growth in fiscal 2028 and called that a supply-constrained figure. "Our demand is much greater than 70%," Huang said on the call, and Kress said supply would remain a bottleneck through at least fiscal 2028.

If demand is that much larger than supply, chips that might otherwise have reached Chinese users through rented capacity are likely to find other buyers. The real risks lie elsewhere: leadership continuity, shareholder lawsuits, a House Republican request that Huang brief lawmakers privately on Nov. 18, and any proof that chips were diverted.

Nvidia reports on Tuesday, Nov. 17, after the close, with Kress leading the company as interim CEO. Investors will be listening for anything about the board probe, Southeast Asian customers, and how Nvidia plans to handle KYC rules if they arrive.
